              Glofitamab: A Summary of Experiments :)

Abstract
Bispecific antibody therapies enable a powerful targeted immune activation against tumor cells, but are
frequently limited by severe toxicities arising from poorly understood links between drug exposure, immune
activation, and downstream cytokine dynamics. We replicated and built on models from many papers, using a
mechanistic and computational framework to study bispecific antibody behavior, with a focus on Glofitamab,
a CD20CD3 T-cell engaging therapy used in lymphoma treatment.
We first conduct a comprehensive literature review spanning three modeling paradigms: full kinetic mass-
action ordinary differential equation (ODE) models, quasi-equilibrium (QE) differential algebraic equation
(DAE) models, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) frameworks. Building on these founda-
tions, we replicate key models using Python and MATLAB/Simulink and construct extended models that
capture multivalent binding, ternary complex formation, and downstream immune side effects.
Our simulations demonstrate that avidity-driven interactions enable sustained target engagement, while en-
gineered affinity asymmetry limits systemic T-cell activation. We further demonstrate that cytokine release
syndrome (CRS) severity is driven primarily by peak ternary complex formation rather than total drug expo-
sure, highlighting the importance of early binding dynamics. In addition, we propose an IL-6 centered weight
loss mechanism in which treatment associated inflammatory signaling contributes to both fat loss and lean
mass loss through anorexia, adipose lipolysis/browning, and skeletal muscle catabolism, distinguishing short
term cytokine driven appetite suppression from longer term cachexia like tissue wasting, providing a basis
for future modeling and experimental validation. Overall, this work establishes a reproducible and mecha-
nistic framework for analyzing bispecific antibody therapies and offers quantitative insights for optimizing
Glofitamab dosing strategies to balance efficacy and toxicity.


1     Background
1.1   Bispecific Antibodies and Glofitamab Mechanism
Bispecific antibodies, such as Glofitamab, represent a rapidly advancing class of targeted therapeutics that
simultaneously bind two distinct targets and enable interaction between immune effector cells and tumor cells.
Glofitamab is a 2:1 bispecific monoclonal antibody commonly used to treat types of B-cell lymphomas, a
class of cancers originating in B lymphocytes that can affect lymph nodes, blood, bone marrow, the central
nervous system, bones, and other tissues. It functions by simultaneously binding CD20 on B cells and CD3 on
T-cells, bringing them into close proximity and creating a ternary complex. This interaction creates a physical
link between tumor cells and T-cells, leading to targeted T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity. However, once formed,


                                                      1
this structure also forces abnormal T-cell activation that bypasses normal mechanisms of immune regulation,
triggering a rapid release of inflammatory cytokines and putting the patient at risk of CRS.


1.2   Clinical Limitation: Cytokine Release Syndrome
As a result, CRS represents a major clinical limitation of T-cell-engaging bispecific antibodies, motivating
the need for predictive and mechanistic modeling approaches. CRS is a systemic inflammatory response
which commonly follows immune-based therapies such as bispecific antibodies. This response arises from
rapid and unregulated immune activation, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Interleuking-
6 (IL-6) often serves as a key clinical biomarker of CRS severity. Previous studies have shown that cytokine
release in CD3 T-cell engager therapies is directly tied to immune activation mechanisms rather than just
drug concentration [1]. Similarly, CAR T-cell modeling work shows that cytokine production depends on
activated immune cell populations and their interactions [2].
After the ternary complex forms, cytokine release happens quickly. IL-6 levels typically rise sharply and then
decrease as the cytokine is cleared from the bloodstream. The peak level of IL-6 is especially important, since
it is closely linked to the severity of CRS. Clinical evidence supports this, noting that “real-time interleukin-6
(IL-6) kinetics predict cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [3]”. Importantly, CRS severity is more strongly
related to the peak level of immune activation (i.e., ternary complex concentration) rather than the total
amount of drug given.

Mechanistically, CRS is initiated as therapeutic agents promote the formation of complexes bridging T-cells

balance efficacy and side effect risk mitigation.


2     Literature Review
2.1   Modeling Frameworks for Antibody Therapy Dynamics
To address these challenges and better predict toxicity and therapeutic efficacy, a range of modeling frame-
works have been developed to describe antibody therapy dynamics. There are three primary classes of mod-
els, each spanning different levels of mechanistic detail and computational complexity. The first classi-
fication consists of full kinetic mass-action ODE models, which explicitly represent cell-surface binding
interactions. These models explicitly represent receptor-ligand association and dissociation, internalization,
recycling, and turnover using coupled ODEs. For bispecific antibodies, this typically includes formation of


                                                      3
binary complexes and ternary complexes. Because all kinetic steps are modeled directly, mass-action sys-
tems can capture transient behaviors such as time dependent target engagement, competition, and saturation.
The second class comprises QE multivalent binding models formulated as DAE systems, which approximate
fast binding dynamics using equilibrium relationships. When binding is fast relative to distribution, internal-
ization, and turnover, we can approximate binding as relatively instantaneous. This replaces the fastest ODE
dynamics with algebraic equilibrium relationships, producing DAEs. These formulations can dramatically
reduce stiffness and parameter sensitivity while still retaining key nonlinearities.
The final class consists of mechanistic PK/PD models for bispecifics. At the whole body scale, and within
the context of this project, compartmental PK/PD frameworks describe antibody absorption, distribution
between plasma and tissues, lymphatic transport, and elimination, while linking these processes to pharma-
codynamic effects such as tumor killing and cytokine release. These models are well suited for dose and
regimen exploration and translation across species and patient populations. However, they require careful
assumptions about tissue structure, permeability, and target expression heterogeneity. Together, these three
approaches underscore the need for integrated frameworks capable of capturing multivalent binding, immune
activation, and clinically relevant outcomes, particularly those associated with CRS.


2.2   Mass-Action ODE Models for Bispecific Binding
Mass-action ODE models provide a detailed mechanistic framework for describing antibody-receptor inter-
actions at the cellular level. Ray et al. [4] developed a mechanistic computational framework to compare
monospecific antibodies with bispecific antibodies that can bind two distinct receptor targets simultaneously,
focusing on a representative bispecific design (BS1) targeting IL-6 and IL-8 receptor systems. Using this
setup, they argue that bispecific binding can produce more efficient functional engagement than monospe-
cific approaches because a single molecule can coordinate interactions across two receptor types rather than
acting independently on one target at a time.
A central contribution of the paper is a kinetic mass-action ODE model of cell-surface receptor–antibody
binding, constructed to explicitly track the full set of interacting species over time.

2.4   Quasi-Equilibrium Multivalent Binding Models
Ng and Bauer [6] utilize a QE binding model which is implemented using DAEs instead of kinetic ODEs. The
authors assume fast binding in kinetics equilibrates instantly in relation to the PK processes. The processes
are then separated into two timescales, slow (in cases such as drug distribution, elimination, and receptor
turnover) and fast (such as drug-receptor binding).
The different ways biologics are able to bind constitute their pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Bi-
ologics refer to complex and different groups of molecules that have diverse binding kinetics to receptors
such as IgG, bispecific antibodies, and antibodies, each with different capabilities as to how many different
receptors they can bind to at a time. Previous models have been created that show how drug receptor binding
occurs and models the drug binding kinetics’ effects on PK and PD behaviors. These models were created
with ODEs with rates of dissociation between drugs and receptors. The rates of dissociation however are
hard to estimate because binding can be quicker than elimination and distribution. Additionally there are
previously constructed QE models, but they have only taken into account drug-receptor interactions that
can only bind one-to-one. Other such models have been created such as quasi-steady-state (QSS), yet each
need intense mathematical derivations and cannot be generalized and applied in different instances without
additional changes to the preexisting models. This study aims to construct a DAE based QE multivalent
drug-receptor binding model to apply to different and complex drug receptor interactions of biologics with
the goals of it being applicable across bispecific antibodies, IgG molecules, and anti-VEGF IgG monoclonal
antibodies, in which each type can bind to different amounts of receptors.


2.5   Structural Design and Mechanism of Action of Bispecific Antibodies
Madsen et al. [7] review the key structural and engineering principles that govern the design of bispecific
antibodies, emphasizing that their therapeutic advantages arise directly from their dual-binding capability.
Bispecific antibodies enable “simultaneous targeting of antigens and synergetic binding effects beyond what
can be obtained even with combinations of conventional monospecific antibodies [7]”. However, this benefit
comes with increased structural complexity compared to monospecific IgGs. The authors note that bispecific
antibody development is frequently challenged by “decreased biophysical stability from fusion of exogenous
antigen-binding domains” and “antibody chain mispairing [leads] to formation of antibody-related impurities
that are very difficult to remove [7]”. As a result, bispecific antibody construction requires careful molecular
engineering to ensure correct chain pairing, structural integrity, and favorable drug-like properties. This
highlights that therapeutic performance is inseparable from structural design and manufacturability.

2.6   Cytokine Dynamics and CRS Mechanisms
Although IL-6 is commonly used as the primary biomarker for CRS, it does not always fully capture the
severity of the immune response. More recent studies suggest that other cytokines, particularly IL-10 and

                                                        9
interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), may be better indicators depending on the therapy being used. For example, one
paper states that “IL-6 trended with CRS for mosunetuzumab tebentafusp and tarlatamab, but IL-10 and
IFN-γ better trended with CRS for glofitamab and elranatamab [8]”. Mechanistically, Glofitamab induces a
rapid pharmacodynamic response that is characterized by early T-cell activation and cytokine release, with
IFN-γ and IL-10 appearing to be the cytokines most closely aligned with CRS onset. In a phase 1 study,
Glofitamab caused dose-dependent induction of inflammatory cytokines right after the first infusion with
IFN-γ peaking at the end of infusion, whereas IL-6 peaks later at approximately six hours after infusion.
IL-10 was also rapidly induced alongside these early cytokines, indicating that it participates in the initial
immune activation phase rather than only a delayed regulatory response. This supports the conclusion that
IFN-γ and IL-10 are a better fit for modeling glofitamab-driven CRS, since these cytokines more directly
reflect early T-cell activation phases rather than the later downstream signal of the macrophage-amplified
inflammatory cascade [8]. This shows that CRS should not be treated as a single-cytokine problem, but
rather as a system involving multiple interacting cytokines.

From a modeling perspective, IL-10 and IFN-γ can be incorporated using a similar structure to IL-6,
with production and clearance terms coupled to immune cell populations. In CAR T-cell models, cytokine
dynamics are described using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that account for both cytokine
production and decay, as well as their relationship to immune activation [2]. In these models, activated T
cells and monocytes are the main drivers of IL-10 and IFN-γ production, which means their levels depend
directly on immune activation. The models also introduce the idea of a combined cytokine burden that
correlates CRS severity, rather than relying on a single cytokine measurement.

These CAR-T based models are useful starting points for bispecific antibody modeling because their
structure can be adapted. For example, CAR-T cell counts can be replaced with activated T-cell populations,
and monocyte dynamics can be modified to represent macrophage or general immune activation states.
Additionally, these models provide reasonable estimates for cytokine production and decay rates, along
with reference concentration ranges for mild versus severe CRS. While the numerical values may differ due
to differences in exposure time between CAR-T and bispecific therapies, general properties such as decay
rates and equation structure can still be applied.
Further understanding of IL-10 and IFN-γ comes from studies of cytokine storms, which focus on how dif-
ferent cytokines interact over time. These studies show that IL-10 and IFN-γ operate on different timescales
and have different roles in the immune response. IFN-γ acts as an early pro-inflammatory driver, increasing
rapidly after immune activation and helping amplify the overall response. In contrast, IL-10 appears later
and functions as part of a regulatory system that reduces inflammation. As described in one study, IFN-γ is
an early driver while IL-10 is part of a later regulatory cluster [9]. IL-10 also has inhibitory effects on other
cytokines, meaning it helps dampen the overall immune response once it has been activated.
This regulatory role of IL-10 is also supported by reduced inflammatory response models. These models
describe anti-inflammatory mediators such as cortisol and IL-10 that suppress pro-inflammatory signaling.
In these frameworks, IL-10 is produced in response to immune activation and tissue damage, and then decays
over time through first-order kinetics. Its main effect is to reduce the activity of pro-inflammatory cytokines,
often through nonlinear inhibition such as Hill-type functions. This type of structure can be directly incor-
porated into CRS models as a feedback mechanism.
In addition to its role in cytokine signaling, IL-10 also affects immune cell behavior. For example, it has been
shown to act as a down regulator of T-cell expansion and macrophage phenotypes, which further supports
its role in limiting excessive immune activation [10]. Overall, this suggests that IL-10 acts as a stabilizing
component in the system, while IFN-γ acts as an amplifying component.


                                                      10
Based on these findings, a more complete CRS model should include both positive and negative mechanisms.
IFN-γ contributes to early immune activation and amplification of cytokine release, while IL-10 provides
delayed negative feedback that helps control and resolve inflammation. Incorporating both cytokines al-
lows the model to better reproduce the characteristic rise-and-fall behavior of cytokine levels during CRS.
However, these additions still require calibration using clinical data, particularly for Glofitamab, in order to
accurately match observed cytokine levels and CRS grading.


2.7   Weight Loss Mechanisms
Weight loss in this system arises from two overlapping sources: the underlying cancer/cachexia state and
treatment associated inflammatory signaling. For modeling purposes, this distinction matters because acute
cytokine elevations during CRS are most likely to capture short term anorexia and sickness behavior, whereas
larger, progressive body weight decline is more consistent with chronic cachexia, which involves ongoing
loss of both fat and lean tissue rather than reduced intake alone [11, 12, 13]. This has also been clinically
supported by a CAR T-cell lymphoma study, which found that recent pre-treatment weight loss was associ-
ated with higher inflammatory markers, including IL-6, and with poorer outcomes, suggesting that weight
loss should be treated as a meaningful inflammatory disease state rather than a trivial side effect [14].
A major mechanism is IL-6 driven skeletal muscle wasting. Chronic IL-6 has been identified as a critical in-
flammatory mediator in cancer cachexia and is strongly linked to loss of muscle mass [15]. More specifically,
it has been shown that IL-6 overexpression can accelerate body weight loss and muscle wasting, while IL-6
receptor blockade can improve mitochondrial content and attenuated progression of cachexia [16]. Mecha-
nistically, this muscle loss pathway involves proteolysis, autophagy, and mitochondrial dysfunction: early
in cachexia, PGC-1α, Mfn1, and Mfn2 are suppressed, and with progressive wasting, FIS1 increases, sup-
porting a shift toward impaired mitochondrial maintenance and a more catabolic muscle state [16]. Thus,
IL-6 is not simply associated with weakness, it plausibly contributes directly to the lean mass component of
weight loss through muscle proteolysis and mitochondrial remodeling [15, 16].
A second major mechanism is IL-6 driven adipose depletion. In a study with cachectic patients, serum IL-6
was positively associated with serum free fatty acids, supporting a link between IL-6 and adipose lipolysis
[12]. This same study found that white adipose tissue lipolysis is elevated in early cachexia, whereas white
adipose browning is more evident in late cachexia, therefore suggesting a progression from early fat mo-
bilization to later thermogenic energy wasting [12]. Importantly, blocking IL-6 signaling with anti-IL-6R
inhibited lipolysis and browning, and partially preserved fat mass in cachectic mice, indicating that IL-6 is
a strong candidate driver of the fat loss component of weight loss [12]. This has been corroborated in the
literature, describing chronic IL-6 as promoting lipolysis, browning, insulin resistance, and negative energy
balance in cachexia [11].
In addition to these tissue effects, the reviewed literature supports a central hypothalamic mechanism that
can further amplify weight loss. Cachexia is described as a state produced by a “synergistic combination”
of decreased appetite and increased metabolism of fat and lean body mass [13]. The hypothalamus contains
anorexigenic POMC/CART neurons and orexigenic AgRP/NPY neurons, and cytokine signaling shifts this
system towards reduced feeding and greater catabolic output [13, 17]. More broadly, hypothalamic inflam-
mation is described as a key driver of anorexia, lethargy, altered energy balance, and downstream catabolism,
with the HPA axis and related neuroendocrine pathways promoting adipose lipolysis and skeletal muscle pro-
teolysis [17]. An IL-6 specific version of this pathway is further supported, as IL-6 can suppress appetite
by inhibiting NPY/AgRP neurons and stimulating POMC neurons while also increasing energy expenditure
[11]. Therefore, any observed weight loss can be understood as the result of both reduced intake and active
inflammatory tissue wasting, not appetite loss or weakness alone [13, 17].

2.8   Summary and Research Gap
Together, these studies demonstrate that bispecific antibody behavior is governed not only by binding affinity,
but also by multivalent interactions, structural geometry, and the separation of biological timescales. Mech-
anistic mass-action models provide detailed descriptions of binding dynamics at the receptor level, while
QE-based approaches improve tractability by approximating fast binding processes. In parallel, PK/PD
models work to capture system-level drug distribution and link exposure to downstream effects.
However, these models fail to capture the full cascade from molecular binding events to clinically relevant
outcomes. In particular, existing models rarely include integrating multivalent binding, immune activation,
and cytokine-mediated toxicity within a unified framework. As a result, the link between early binding
dynamics and downstream clinical effects such as CRS remain insufficiently characterized.
This gap motivates the development of an integrated framework capable of connecting receptor-level interac-
tions, pharmacokinetics, and immune-driven toxicity, enabling a more complete understanding of bispecific
antibody therapies.


3     Methodology
3.1   Model Selection and Framework
Our methodology is designed to be Python-heavy, reproducible, and directly useful for building and ana-
lyzing candidate models for Glofitamab. We collected peer reviewed literature covering the three types of
models described in the introduction, and from each selected work we analyzed and replicated the mecha-
nistic models including state variables, processes, parameters, and assumptions.
We then replicated the models in our literature review with a Python implementation. To formalize this pro-
cess, we replicated representative mechanistic models from the literature and validated our implementations


                                                      13
by reproducing published results. With these results in mind, we conducted further research and developed a
best candidate model for Glofitamab that can mechanistically explain real world effects. Simulink was used
to model and test the results of clinical side effects for this drug.
Using the model, effects such as infusion reactions and cytokine disturbances can then be tested and simulated
in vitro. Our research seeks to analyze and minimize the most common effects such as CRS and fatigue.


3.2   Mass-Action Binding Model Replication
To validate the mass-action modeling framework, we replicated the BS1 bispecific antibody model from [4].
All simulations were implemented in MATLAB Simulink using continuous-time ODE integration, with a
Step block imposing antibody washout at t = 2 hours. The BS1 model describes a 1:1 bispecific antibody
targeting IL-6R and IL-8R via sequential binding: monovalent engagement of one receptor forms a binary
complex, which then cross-links to the second to form a stable ternary complex. Three species are tracked

3.5   Cytokine-PK/PD Coupled Modeling Framework
From a modeling standpoint, CRS reflects the fact that different biological processes occur on different
timescales. Binding and ternary complex formation happen quickly (within minutes to hours), while phar-
macokinetics, such as drug distribution and clearance, occur more slowly (over days). Because of this, many
models assume quasi-equilibrium binding, where receptor interactions are treated as fast compared to PK
processors [6]. Cytokine production is then modeled as a nonlinear function of the ternary complex, while
cytokine clearance is typically first-order.

4.2   Replication of BS1 Binding Model
During the binding phase (0–2 hours), the ternary complex (IL-6R-BS1-IL-8R, orange) saturated near 3.1 ×
105 complexes/cell, mirroring the IL-6R ceiling of 3.16 × 105 #/cell, which confirmed IL-6R as the limiting
receptor. The binary IL-8R complex (BS1-IL-8R, blue) accumulated steadily thereafter as excess antibodies
engaged the more abundant IL-8R pool. The binary IL-6R complex (BS1-IL-6R, yellow) remained near
zero throughout, as any IL-6R-bound antibody rapidly completed cross-linking before a stable binary species
could accumulate.
Upon washout at 2 hours, the binary IL-8R complex decayed while the ternary complex exhibited a brief
upward inflection before plateauing — a direct manifestation of the avidity effect, wherein dissociating
binary complexes release free receptors that tethered antibodies exploit to complete cross-linking. The result
is sustained ternary occupancy in the complete absence of free drug, with implications for therapeutic activity
beyond the pharmacokinetic window.

4.3   Dose-dependent Binding Behavior
To extend the BS1 replication beyond the four-hour time-course shown in Figure 11, we analyzed how
initial BS1 concentration affected final receptor occupancy and ternary complex formation under a fixed
receptor condition of IL-6R = 50 and IL-8R = 50. The simulations showed that total bound receptor in-
creased monotonically with BS1 dose and approached saturation near the total available receptor pool, while
ternary complex formation followed a bell-shaped dose-response, reaching a maximum at an intermediate
concentration and declining at higher doses. This indicates that greater overall receptor occupancy does
not necessarily correspond to greater productive bispecific binding. Instead, the linked two-receptor state is
maximized within an intermediate dose window, while very high BS1 concentrations increasingly favor one-
sided binary binding over ternary complex formation. These dose-response results complement the original
BS1 time-course replication by showing that, in addition to avidity and post-washout persistence, concentra-
tion strongly shapes the balance between total target engagement and the functionally important bispecific
complex. This is directly relevant to the broader Glofitamab framework, where the analogous functional
objective is not simply more binding, but formation of the productive bridged state between target B-cells
and effector T-cells.

4.4   Glofitamab Binding Dynamics (2:1 Architecture)
During the binding phase (0–2 hours), the high-affinity CD20 arms drove rapid B-cell coating: the monova-
lent B-cell complex (yellow) peaked near 8.7 × 104 , and the bivalent anchor (blue) stabilized near 4.8 × 104
with notably flat kinetics once formed. The monovalent T-cell complex (orange) also accumulated given
the 100 nM dose exceeding the CD3 K_d, but these represent transient solution-phase contacts rather than
productive synaptic events. Accordingly, the fully locked synapse (purple) remained low throughout the
infusion, rising only gradually — reflecting the geometric constraint of physically bridging two cell types
and constituting the key safety mechanism limiting acute T-cell hyperactivation.
Upon washout at 2 hours, the monovalent T-cell complex (orange) decayed sharply while the bivalent CD20
anchor (blue) remained almost entirely stable — a direct consequence of cooperative bivalent binding pre-
venting full dissociation. Strikingly, both synapse species (green and purple) continued to increase post-
washout: antibodies anchored to B-cells via both CD20 arms efficiently captured CD3 receptors freed by
the decay of monovalent complexes, forming additional synapses without any free drug present. This post-
washout synapse growth mirrors the avidity effect observed in the BS1 model and provides a mechanistic
basis for Glofitamab’s sustained anti-tumor activity beyond its pharmacokinetic window.

4.5   Comparative Analysis of 1:1 versus 2:1 Architectures
Both models converge on the same core finding: avidity-driven complex stabilization allows therapeutic
bridging activity to outlast free drug availability, with the highest-order complexes increasing post-washout
as weaker monovalent species break apart and donate free receptors.
The 2:1 Glofitamab architecture adds two clinically meaningful refinements. First, bivalent CD20 anchoring
pre-positions the antibody at the tumor site before any T-cell contact, creating a B-cell sink that concentrates
activity where it is needed. Second, weak CD3 affinity gates T-cell activation on tumor co-engagement,
substantially reducing systemic cytokine risk. These properties are encoded directly in the model parameters
and their mechanistic consequences — sustained synapse formation and CRS mitigation — are observable
in the simulation dynamics and will be explored further in the Discussion.

5     Discussion
5.1   Role of Cytokine Feedback in CRS Dynamics
Across our simulations, including ternary complex formation, cytokine dose-response curves (IFN-γ, IL-6,
IL-2, IL-10), and clinical readouts (fever and fatigue), CRS sensitivity is driven by the balance of positive
and negative feedback mechanisms. Early T-cell activation and ternary complex formation trigger IFN-
γ–mediated amplification, rapidly escalating pro-inflammatory cytokines and activating macrophages and
monocytes, as reflected in peak IL-6 and IFN-γ curves. In contrast, IL-10 functions as a negative feedback
regulator, and its timely induction suppresses excessive cytokine signaling, stabilizing fever and limiting
fatigue despite dose variations, as shown in the purple fatigue curves and fever dynamics plots. These
integrated simulation outputs highlight how feedback timing and strength shape both molecular and physio-
logical responses, providing mechanistic insight into CRS onset and severity.
This understanding has direct clinical implications for bispecific antibody therapy. When positive feedback
dominates, even small increases in ternary complex formation can provoke disproportionately large cytokine
responses and severe CRS. Incorporating negative feedback mechanisms, either naturally through IL-10
induction or strategically through interventions like dose titration or step-up regimens, can prevent runaway
cytokine responses, reduce peak levels, and keep clinical symptoms in check. Linking cytokine dynamics
to symptom curves provides a clear framework for predicting CRS and optimizing treatment to reduce risk
while keeping therapy effective.


5.2   Model Limitations and Assumptions
Despite the mechanistic detail incorporated in this framework, several important limitations should be ac-
knowledged. Many parameter values were either assumed or drawn from literature under varying experi-
mental conditions, which may not fully reflect the specific biological context of Glofitamab as a targeted
therapeutic drug. Consequently, model outputs should be interpreted primarily as relative trends and mech-
anistic insights, rather than precise patient-level predictions.
In addition, the current model does not account for interpatient variability. Biological heterogeneity in factors
such as receptor expression, immune cell abundance, and cytokine response has the potential to substantially
influence clinical outcomes. The absence of this variability limits the model’s ability to capture the full
spectrum of possible observed patient responses. Furthermore, this works represents the immune system
through a simplified lens, omitting complex and key regulatory pathways. Thus, this work has a limited
ability to fully reproduce the complexity of factors affecting immune activation and dysregulation during
antibody therapies.
Taken together, these limitations highlight that the proposed framework is best interpreted as a mechanistic
and hypothesis-generating model, rather than a fully predictive clinical tool. However, these limitations also
provide clear directions for future work and improvement. Addressing these constraints through incorpora-
tion of patient-specific variability, integration of spatially resolved modeling approaches, and refinement of



                                                       28
parameter estimation using clinical datasets will be essential for advancing model fidelity. In particular, im-
proving the representation of immune heterogeneity and toxicity dynamics will be critical for capturing the
complexity of clinical outcomes. The present framework serves as a foundational framework for which more
physiologically realistic and clinically actionable models of bispecific antibody therapy can be developed.


5.3   Future Directions
Building on the limitations identified in this work, future efforts should focus on extending the current frame-
work into a more physiologically realistic and clinically predictive model. While the present work captures
key features of multivalent binding, ternary complex formation, cytokine release, and hypothalamic temper-
ature dysregulation, several important directions remain for further development. In addition, weight loss
mechanisms were examined conceptually through literature review, but were not explicitly implemented
or tested in the current framework, and thus remain an important direction for future model development,
simulation, and validation.
A primary area for improvement is the incorporation of patient specific variability in receptor expression,
baseline cytokine levels, and drug clearance. These factors are very likely to drive significant differences in
CRS severity and treatment across individuals. Incorporating such variability, alongside calibration against
clinical datasets will also strengthen parameter identifiability and improve confidence in the predictions of
the model.
Another important direction is the refinement of downstream toxicity modeling, particularly for fatigue.
Rather than relying on a direct dependence on dose alone, future models should investigate whether fa-
tigue is better explained by cumulative inflammatory burden, prolonged cytokine exposure, or downstream
metabolic stress. It could also be useful to include threshold effects, lag dynamics, and recovery compart-
ments since fatigue often appears as a delayed and persistent symptom rather than an immediate acute re-
sponse.
Finally, future studies should leverage this expanded framework to evaluate optimized dosing strategies that
balance therapeutic efficacy with toxicity mitigation. Such efforts will enable rational design of dosing
regimens that minimize CRS risk while preserving anti-tumor efficacy, ultimately supporting the translation
of mechanistic modeling approaches into clinically actionable tools.


5.4   Further Model Testing and Simulation
Further testing of the model should focus on systematically exploring how dosing design changes the bal-
ance between efficacy and toxicity. Additional simulation should target the downstream toxicity modules,
and the hypothalamic temperature model should be challenged under a wider range of cytokine profiles to
determine whether the timing and magnitude of simulated fever remain physiologically reasonable across
mild, moderate, and severe inflammatory scenarios.
Likewise, the fatigue model requires further testing because the current results indicate that fatigue did not
change by a large amount when dose size or spacing was varied. Simulations for further research should
test alternative fatigue structures tied to cumulative cytokine response, duration of fever, repeated T-cell
activation, or delayed inflammatory recovery. These results can then be compared to determine which model
best reproduces clinically plausible trends.
Finally, the model could also be further tested with validation oriented simulation studies that compare out-
puts across the linked submodules. Testing should confirm whether predictions remain internally consistent
across short timescale binding dynamics and also longer timescale outcomes. Any additions should conform


                                                      29
to the existing findings or provide other mechanistic explanations. In this way, further model testing will
improve confidence in the integrated framework and help refine it into a predictive tool for treatment design.
